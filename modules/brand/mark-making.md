# Mark making: how a logo represents an idea

Reference for `logo.md` (LOGO-1 to LOGO-16). Load when choosing what a mark should show and how far to abstract it, when reducing a drawing to a mark, or when building and correcting one. Double readings and mashups: `clever-marks.md`. Famous marks are cited examples only: describe them, never draw or reproduce them (BRAND-4, BRAND-8). To look a mark up, Wikimedia Commons keeps logo categories with dates and sources [SRC-410]; link to it, don't copy from it. Grades follow `core/evidence.md`.

## 1. What to show: the abstraction ladder

Every mark sits somewhere on one ladder. Pick the rung on purpose (LOGO-15).

| Rung | What it is | Cited examples | Use when | Watch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Literal** | the thing itself, simplified | a penguin for Penguin Books [SRC-395]; a shell for Shell [SRC-399] | the name is a picture; a new brand needs to say what it is | the obvious picture is usually crowded (LOGO-2) |
| **Associative** | something linked to the subject, one step over | a crocodile for a tennis player nicknamed "the Crocodile" [SRC-405]; a tongue for a rock band, drawn from a goddess image and the singer's mouth [SRC-404] | the story behind the name is the ownable part | the link must be findable, or it reads as random |
| **Metaphorical** | a picture of a quality, not a thing | a curved stroke for motion [SRC-397]; a panda for "endangered and loved" [SRC-396]; a heart for "love" [SRC-403] | the quality matters more than the product | the metaphor must survive without a caption |
| **Abstract** | a shape with no subject; meaning is given by use | a square inside an octagon for a bank [SRC-400] | broad or changing businesses; long horizons | needs years of use to fill with meaning; hardest to own |
| **Letterform** | the name's own letters made into an image | see section 4 | long or generic names; app icons | letters alone carry little meaning |

**Evidence.** Across studies, logos that show what the business does (descriptive) raised brand evaluations and purchase intent, more so for unfamiliar brands [SRC-361]: a reason to sit nearer the literal end when a brand is new. Abstract marks earn their meaning over time: the octagon was radical in 1960 because few large American firms used abstract marks, and advertising filled it in [SRC-400]. Rand put it bluntly: a mark takes its meaning from the thing it stands for, not the other way round [SRC-342]. Grade: partly supported for descriptiveness; craft wisdom for the rest.

**Meaning can be many.** A mark can carry a family of meanings if one shape holds them: a consumer-goods U built from 25 small icons, each a product area or value [SRC-406]. It works as a large badge; at favicon size it is one U. Treat the icons as a second-look reward, not the identity.

## 2. From sketch or photo to mark: reduction

Most loved figurative marks were drawn from the real thing, then cut back.

- **Draw from life first.** The penguin began with a young designer sent to a zoo to sketch penguins, later redrawn more than once, including a cleaner version by Jan Tschichold [SRC-395]. The panda began with sketches of a zoo panda (Gerald Watterson), from which Peter Scott drew the first mark [SRC-396].
- **Cut to the silhouette.** Keep what names the subject (the panda's eye patches, the scallop's ribs and fan) and drop what describes one individual. Black and white first: the panda was chosen partly because it prints well in one colour [SRC-396].
- **Reduce over decades, not in one go.** The shell went from a realistic scallop to a flat fan of ribs (Raymond Loewy, 1971) and later dropped its word [SRC-399]; the siren lost its ring and wordmark in 2011, leaving the figure alone [SRC-398]. Each step removed one layer once the layer beneath was well known (LOGO-12).
- **Stop at one distinctive feature.** Overly simple marks are harder to own and, in one study, less memorable than moderately elaborate ones [SRC-359]. Reduction ends when one more cut would lose the feature people recognise.

**Method.** Photograph or draw the subject from several angles; trace the strongest silhouette; redraw it in 3 to 5 weights of detail; test each at 16 px and as a 1 cm stamp (LOGO-3); keep the simplest that still reads as *this* subject, not the category. Grade: craft wisdom with documented cases.

## 3. Gesture and stance: figurative marks with character

An animal or figure carries mood through pose more than detail.

- **Choose the verb.** Running, leaping, sitting, looking up: the pose is the message. A crocodile drawn side-on, mouth open, reads as tenacity [SRC-405]; a penguin standing upright reads as poise.
- **Weight and direction.** Forward lean and rightward motion read as progress in left-to-right cultures; a still, symmetrical pose reads as stable. Marks that imply motion were judged more dynamic and engaging [SRC-365].
- **One posture, many uses.** A mascot needs a primary pose that works as a mark, plus a few secondary poses for the system (LOGO-5, LOGO-14).
- **Expression from minimum parts.** One open mouth and a tongue made a band's mark: drawn by student John Pasche after Mick Jagger showed him an image of the goddess Kali, now in the V&A [SRC-404]. Two dots and a curve can carry a face; more parts dilute it.

## 4. Letterforms, monograms and the character of a wordmark

- **Letter as picture.** The letters can become the object: a lowercase m and b drawn as a baseball glove holding a ball, by an art student, chosen from more than 2,000 contest entries [SRC-389].
- **Monograms.** Two or three letters fused into one shape; the joins are where the ownership is. Look at the counters (enclosed spaces) as hard as the strokes.
- **Rebus.** Swap a word for a picture: the heart in a famous tourism mark, first sketched by Milton Glaser in a taxi, sketches now held by MoMA [SRC-403].
- **Wordmark character.** The name's voice comes from a few choices: weight, width, contrast, terminals (round, sharp, flared), case, spacing and one custom move. Name the quality first (LOGO-7), then pick the letterform traits that carry it. Broken or incomplete letters raise interest but can cut trust [SRC-363].
- **Symmetry tricks.** An ambigram reads the same after rotation: a 1982 computer-company mark spells its three-letter name four ways round a square [SRC-390]. Powerful but rare; it works only when the letters allow it.

## 5. Emblems, badges and crests

- **Container plus content.** A shape (shield, roundel, circle, ribbon) frames a symbol and words. Good for heritage, institutions, clubs, food and drink.
- **Build in layers, ship a core.** Design the full badge and the small version together: the core symbol must stand alone at 16 px (LOGO-3). The siren is the case of a badge whose core outgrew the ring [SRC-398].
- **Watch:** too much text at small size, generic shields, fake founding dates.

## 6. Construction: grids, circles, ratios and the eye

- **Circles and grids are a tool, not a proof.** The 2012 social-network bird was built from overlapping circles; its designer describes the geometry as a way to make the curves consistent [SRC-407]. Use a grid to start and the eye to finish (LOGO-6).
- **The golden ratio is mostly post-hoc.** Many "golden" overlays are drawn after the fact, with thick lines that fit almost anything; claims about a famous fruit logo have been debunked, and there is no good evidence people prefer the ratio [SRC-408]. Use it if it helps you decide; never sell it as the reason a mark works. Grade: myth when presented as the cause.
- **Optical corrections.** Round shapes overshoot flat ones; horizontals are drawn thinner than verticals; crossings are thinned to stop dark spots; centres are set optically, not by measurement (LOGO-6, SRC-265).
- **Balance stroke and space.** Treat the gaps as shapes. Even, deliberate negative space reads as quality; one study found that adding active space between a pictorial logo's parts improved how it was rated, most for sophisticated and sincere brands, and not for rugged ones [SRC-381]. Grade: partly supported.

## 7. Why some marks are remembered and loved

- **One distinctive feature, used for years.** Recognition comes from consistent use more than design alone [SRC-120]; the swoosh was made by a student for $35 and its founder was lukewarm at first [SRC-397].
- **Natural, moderately elaborate, harmonious** designs were recognised best in a 195-logo study [SRC-359].
- **Easy to process** feels good: fluency raises liking [SRC-37].
- **A story people can retell** (drawn from life, a taxi sketch, a nickname) helps a mark spread, but check the story: some are disputed. The wool mark is officially credited to Francesco Saroglia, yet sketches and diaries support Franco Grignani entering under that name [SRC-402]; the "barn hex sign" origin of a TV network's eye is partly apocryphal, and a Shaker drawing reproduced in a magazine is the better-supported source [SRC-401]. The "bite for byte" and computer-pioneer stories behind a fruit logo are denied by its designer [SRC-409].

## Process summary

Brief and word map (LOGO-1) → choose the rung (LOGO-15) → draw from the real thing → 20+ thumbnails across routes → reduce to silhouettes → test small and in one colour (LOGO-3, LOGO-4) → construct and correct optically (LOGO-6) → one clever move only if it is found, not forced (`clever-marks.md`, LOGO-16) → test in the world (LOGO-13). Practitioners show the same breadth: one designer posts that he draws 15 options for every logo [SRC-412].

## Failure modes

- **Too literal and crowded:** the category's usual picture (LOGO-2).
- **Too abstract too soon:** a shape with no story and no budget to fill it.
- **Over-reduced:** loses the one feature that made it this subject.
- **Detail that dies small:** fine lines, thin gaps, tiny text.
- **Geometry as excuse:** a construction diagram defending a mark that doesn't read.
- **Story without substance:** a nice origin myth on a weak mark.
