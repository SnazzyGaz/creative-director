# Logo design: idea first, rules as the edit pass (LOGO-1 to LOGO-16)

Load with the brand README when the job is to make or change a logo, symbol, monogram, badge or mascot mark (not to place an existing one: that is BRAND-4). Format: **What / Why / Check / Fix / Grade / Source**. LOGO-11 is **MUST-BE-RIGHT**; the rest are defaults. Types, moods, trends, case lessons and reading: `logo-notes.md`. What to show and how to reduce and build it: `mark-making.md`. Double readings and mashups: `clever-marks.md`.

**How to use these.** The idea comes first (LOGO-1, BRAND-9); the rest are the edit pass that makes an idea survive real use. Most of the rules are the long consensus of practitioners: Rand, Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv, Airey, Wheeler, Mollerup, Bierut and Draplin agree on the core: simple, distinctive, appropriate, works small and in one colour [SRC-339, SRC-340, SRC-341, SRC-342]. Where a study speaks to a claim, the grade says so.

---

### LOGO-1. Idea first: brief, word map, many thumbnails, then pick
- **What:** Before drawing anything final: (1) the brief, with the logo questions in `core/brand-intake.md`; (2) a word map: the literal subject, the name's sounds and letters, two verbs for what the brand does, the one quality it must carry (LOGO-7) and its opposite; (3) at least 20 small black-and-white thumbnails or silhouettes across at least four routes (letter, picture, abstract, combination); (4) pick two or three by LOGO-2, LOGO-3 and BRAND-9; only then refine.
- **Why:** Practitioners describe the same front end: research and a written brief, then sketching many ideas small and fast before committing [SRC-339, SRC-348, SRC-349]. Refining the first idea skips the search where the distinctive answer usually is.
- **Check:** Is there a word map and a sheet of 20 or more thumbnails covering four routes? Does each finalist trace to a word on the map?
- **Fix:** Go back to the map; push the routes that are missing.
- **Grade:** Procedure (craft wisdom behind it).
- **Source:** Airey [SRC-339]; Draplin [SRC-348]; Bierut [SRC-349]; BRAND-9; CD-1.

### LOGO-2. Simple enough to remember, distinctive enough to own
- **What:** One idea, few parts, a clear silhouette, and a shape not shared with the category's usual marks (globes, swooshes, bulbs, leaves, generic shields).
- **Why:** Haviv's three tests are appropriate, distinctive and simple, and he notes simplicity and distinctiveness pull against each other: the simpler the mark, the harder to own [SRC-341]. Airey's traits add memorability and "focus on one thing" [SRC-339]. In a study of 195 logos, designs that were natural, harmonious and moderately elaborate were recognised best; very simple ones were less memorable [SRC-359].
- **Check:** (a) Describe it in one sentence; (b) after a 5-second look, someone sketches it from memory with the main feature right; (c) silhouettes of 8 to 10 category marks beside it: is it the odd one out?
- **Fix:** Cut parts until one feature carries it; if it now looks generic, give that feature one specific twist.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom; partly supported for moderate elaboration [SRC-359].
- **Source:** Haviv [SRC-341]; Airey [SRC-339]; Henderson & Cote [SRC-359].

### LOGO-3. Works at the smallest real size, with a small-size version if needed
- **What:** The mark reads at 16 and 32 px (favicon, tab, app icon), on a 1 cm stamp and across a building. Fine detail, thin strokes and small counters that fill in are drawn out, or a simplified small version is part of the system.
- **Why:** "Think small" is a core trait [SRC-339]. Responsive logos simplify step by step as space shrinks [SRC-353]. Search engines show favicons from a square image, recommended larger than 48 px [SRC-368].
- **Check:** Render at 16, 32 and 48 px and at 1 cm wide in print. Does it still read as itself, with no merged strokes or filled counters? Strokes at least about 1 px at 16 px.
- **Fix:** Thicken strokes, open counters, drop secondary parts; draw a dedicated small version (often the symbol or initial alone). When thickening, keep the silhouette and proportions that carry the idea: a long mark should stay long. Making it short and heavy turns it into a different object.
- **Grade:** Standard for the platform sizes; craft wisdom for the detail limits.
- **Source:** Airey [SRC-339]; Harrison [SRC-353]; Google Search Central [SRC-368]; UI-5.

### LOGO-4. One colour first, reversed second, full colour last
- **What:** The mark is finished in solid black on white before colour is chosen, and works as white on black and in one ink (embroidery, fax, foil, etching, a stamp). Colour, gradients and texture are additions, never what holds it together.
- **Why:** Long practitioner consensus: a mark that needs colour or effects to read fails in half its uses [SRC-339, SRC-340]. The Logobook archive shows every mark in black and white, so form alone has to carry it [SRC-377].
- **Check:** Black only, white on black, one mid-tone ink: does each read, with no part lost? Does the mark still hold if gradients are flattened?
- **Fix:** Rebuild the form in solid shapes; use colour after, by role (BRAND-2).
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom.
- **Source:** Airey [SRC-339]; CG&H [SRC-340]; Logobook [SRC-377].

### LOGO-5. A system, not a single file: lockups, clear space, minimum size
- **What:** Deliver the symbol, the wordmark, a horizontal and a stacked lockup, the small version (LOGO-3), one-colour and reversed files, a clear-space unit (a part of the mark, such as the cap height) and a minimum size. Symbol and wordmark share stroke, corner and proportion so they look born together.
- **Why:** Brand standards specify exactly these parts so the mark survives other people's layouts [SRC-119]. Identities now act as toolkits around the mark [SRC-358].
- **Check:** Are all the parts present, with the unit and minimum written down? Overlay symbol strokes on the wordmark: same weight and corners?
- **Fix:** Draw the missing lockups; match weights; write the one-page usage sheet (format in `core/brand-intake.md`).
- **Grade:** Standard (brand practice).
- **Source:** Wheeler [SRC-119]; LogoLounge 2026 [SRC-358]; BRAND-4.

### LOGO-6. Grid to start, eye to finish
- **What:** Build on circles and a grid if it helps, then correct by eye: rounds overshoot, horizontals thinner than verticals, joins thinned so they do not blob, visual centre slightly above the geometric one, balanced weight in the counters.
- **Why:** Equal geometry does not look equal; icon systems and type designers correct for it [SRC-265]. A construction grid is a means, not the proof.
- **Check:** Flip the mark upside down and mirror it; blur it: any part heavier, lower or lumpier than meant? Rounds at the same height as flats look smaller?
- **Fix:** Apply the corrections in COMP-24 and TYPE-14; trust the flipped view over the grid.
- **Grade:** Standard (optics; type and icon practice).
- **Source:** Material Design keylines [SRC-265]; COMP-24; TYPE-14.

### LOGO-7. Name the quality, then choose the shapes that carry it
- **What:** Write one or two qualities the mark must carry (trust, speed, warmth, precision...). Choose shape, line, angle, symmetry, weight and type for them using the mood map in `logo-notes.md`, and say which move carries which quality.
- **Why:** Some mappings have evidence: round forms read soft and comforting and angular ones hard and durable [SRC-360]; asymmetric marks feel more exciting and suit exciting brands [SRC-362]; static marks that imply movement read as more dynamic and engaging [SRC-365]; broken letterforms read innovative but less trustworthy [SRC-363]. The effects are small and depend on context; most of the map is craft.
- **Check:** Three people (or a fresh reviewer) give three words for the mark without the brief: does at least one match the target quality? Can each design move be traced to a quality?
- **Fix:** Change the strongest carrier first: the outline (round or angular), then the angle and symmetry, then the type.
- **Grade:** Partly supported for roundness, symmetry, implied motion and broken type [SRC-360, SRC-362, SRC-363, SRC-365]; the rest craft wisdom.
- **Source:** Jiang et al. [SRC-360]; Luffarelli et al. [SRC-362]; Hagtvedt [SRC-363]; Cian et al. [SRC-365]; `logo-notes.md`.

### LOGO-8. Clever reads second, never instead
- **What:** The mark works at a glance without its trick; a hidden or double reading is a reward, not the message. Check for readings you did not mean.
- **Why:** The best-known negative-space arrow sits in a wordmark that looks plain until it is spotted; its designer drew custom letters so the arrow fits without distorting them, and many viewers only find it when told. The logo works either way [SRC-354, SRC-355]. The 2026 trend report calls objects plugged into letter counters "occasionally forced" [SRC-358]. Launches show unintended readings spread fast [SRC-372, SRC-373].
- **Check:** Cover the trick: does the mark still read and please? Explain it in one sentence; if it needs a paragraph, it fails. Rotate it 90 and 180 degrees, look at it small and from across a room: any rude, violent or hateful shape?
- **Fix:** Simplify the trick until it reads in one sentence, or drop it and keep the stronger plain form.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom with cited cases.
- **Source:** Logo Design Love [SRC-354]; Creative Review [SRC-355]; LogoLounge [SRC-358]; Airbnb and London 2012 cases [SRC-372, SRC-373].

### LOGO-9. Pick the logo type for the name, the budget and the life
- **What:** Choose the type deliberately (`logo-notes.md`): a wordmark for a short distinctive name or a new brand with little media money; an initial when the name is long; a symbol when the mark must work without words and there is time and spend to teach it; a combination as the safe default; an emblem where heritage and fixed settings rule; a mascot where character and story are the asset.
- **Why:** A symbol carries meaning only once people link it to the name [SRC-342, SRC-120]. Descriptive marks, which show what the business does, helped unfamiliar brands in studies, less so familiar ones, and hurt in categories people feel bad about [SRC-361].
- **Check:** Write the type and one line on why it fits the name, budget and settings. New, unknown brand with an abstract symbol alone? Flag it.
- **Fix:** Add the name in a lockup, or a descriptive cue, until the brand is known.
- **Grade:** Partly supported for descriptiveness [SRC-361]; craft wisdom for the rest.
- **Source:** Rand [SRC-342]; Romaniuk [SRC-120]; Luffarelli, Mukesh & Mahmood [SRC-361]; Mollerup [SRC-343].

### LOGO-10. Trends are seasoning; the mark stands without them
- **What:** If the mark uses a current trend (a gradient, a slant cut, a dazzle star), name it and remove it: the idea must still stand. Prefer forms that have lasted (simple geometry, drawn letters) for the core and put trends in the changeable layers (colour, motion, pattern).
- **Why:** Trend reports present themselves as reports, not recommendations [SRC-358]. "Incorporate tradition" and avoiding trend-led marks is a standing practitioner rule [SRC-339]; marks from 1940 to 1980 built on plain geometry still look current [SRC-346].
- **Check:** List any trend moves (see the trend list in `logo-notes.md`). Remove them: does the idea survive? Would it look dated in ten years?
- **Fix:** Move the trend out of the mark into the system's flexible parts.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom.
- **Source:** LogoLounge [SRC-356, SRC-357, SRC-358]; Airey [SRC-339]; Müller [SRC-346].

### LOGO-11. Not confusable, and registrable (flag to the owner)
- **What:** The mark must not look like an existing mark in the same or a nearby field. Its distinctive part should not be only a generic picture of the product or a plain descriptive word.
- **Why:** A trademark exists to tell one trader's goods from another's; generic and merely descriptive signs are weak or unregistrable [SRC-369], and a confusingly similar sign infringes [SRC-171].
- **Check:** Reverse-image search the finalists and look at the category's marks; list the closest three and how this differs. Is the core more than a picture of the product?
- **Fix:** Change the silhouette, not a detail. A close likeness is flagged to the owner of the work in the hand-off, with the three nearest marks, rather than treated as a blocker: whether it is too close, and any legal search, is their call.
- **Grade:** Standard (trade-mark law).
- **Source:** WIPO [SRC-369]; Trade Marks Act 1994 s.10 [SRC-171]; BRAND-8.

### LOGO-12. Redesigns keep the equity
- **What:** Before changing a known mark, list its distinctive assets (shape, colour, letter, character) and keep at least the strongest; evolve rather than replace, unless the brief is a deliberate break, and then say what is given up.
- **Why:** Loyal customers dislike logo redesigns more than others do [SRC-364]. Assets work through recognition built over time [SRC-120]. A pack redesign that dropped its known image was followed by a reported fall of about 20% in sales and a reversal [SRC-370]; a clothing retailer dropped a new logo within a week [SRC-371].
- **Check:** Is there an asset list, with what is kept? Shown to someone who knows the old mark: do they still know whose it is?
- **Fix:** Bring back the strongest asset and simplify towards it.
- **Grade:** Partly supported [SRC-364, SRC-120]; cases are illustrative.
- **Source:** Walsh et al. [SRC-364]; Romaniuk [SRC-120]; Tropicana [SRC-370]; Gap [SRC-371].

### LOGO-13. Test in the world before calling it done
- **What:** The finalist passes a test sheet: a size ladder (16 px to poster), squint or blur, black and white and reversed, and five mockups where it will really live (app icon or avatar crop in a circle, sign, document header, merchandise or stamp, a busy photo background).
- **Why:** Practitioners test marks across avatars, embroidery and shirts before presenting [SRC-348]; launch reactions often turn on context [SRC-373].
- **Check:** Is there one test sheet with all of these? Does the circle crop cut anything vital?
- **Fix:** Fix the failing case in the mark or add a variant (LOGO-3, LOGO-5).
- **Grade:** Procedure.
- **Source:** Draplin [SRC-348]; London 2012 [SRC-373]; LOGO-3 to LOGO-5.

### LOGO-14. Dynamic identities keep one constant
- **What:** A changing or generative identity keeps at least one fixed element people can learn (a shape, a letter, a grid, a colour), and the variants follow written rules. Any animated version avoids flashing more than three times a second.
- **Why:** A research lab's algorithmic, ever-changing mark was followed by a fixed monogram on the same seven-by-seven grid, with a related glyph for each of its 23 groups [SRC-350]; a museum's zigzag W changes shape but is always the same line [SRC-352]. A launch film was withdrawn over seizure reports [SRC-373]; WCAG limits flashes for the same reason.
- **Check:** Name the constant. Shown three variants, does a stranger see one brand? Flash count under three per second?
- **Fix:** Fix one element; reduce the variables; slow the motion.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom with cases; standard for flashing (WCAG 2.3.1).
- **Source:** Pentagram MIT Media Lab [SRC-350]; Experimental Jetset Whitney [SRC-352]; London 2012 [SRC-373].

### LOGO-15. Choose the level of abstraction on purpose, and reduce from the real thing
- **What:** Decide where the mark sits: literal, associative, metaphorical, abstract or letterform. For a figure, draw the real subject first (life, photos, several angles), then cut to the silhouette and the one feature that names it.
- **Why:** Logos that show what the business does raised brand evaluations, most for unfamiliar brands [SRC-361]; abstract marks fill with meaning only through years of use [SRC-400, SRC-342]. Many lasting figurative marks began as drawings from life and were reduced over time [SRC-395, SRC-396, SRC-399].
- **Check:** Can you name the rung and say why it suits this brand's age and budget? For a figure, is there reference drawing behind it, and does the reduced mark still read as this subject, not the category, at 16 px?
- **Fix:** New brand with a vague abstract mark: move one rung towards the literal. Stiff or generic figure: go back to reference and redraw the pose.
- **Grade:** Partly supported for descriptiveness [SRC-361]; craft wisdom with cases for the rest.
- **Source:** Luffarelli et al. [SRC-361]; Rand [SRC-342]; cases [SRC-395, SRC-396, SRC-399, SRC-400]; `mark-making.md`.

### LOGO-16. A second reading must be identifiable and meaningful once seen
- **What:** In a double-reading mark, the hidden figure must be easy to see once pointed out, and must mean something about the brand. Never count on it working unnoticed.
- **Why:** In a 2026 set of studies, figure-ground reversal logos raised brand attitude only when the hidden object could be identified and interpreted [SRC-378]; a famous hidden arrow did not steer attention unconsciously, and most viewers did not report it [SRC-379].
- **Check:** Show five people for two seconds: all name the main reading. Point out the second: each sees it unaided and can say why it fits. At the smallest size, the main reading survives even if the second fades.
- **Fix:** Enlarge or open the hidden shape; cut a hidden figure that has no link to the brand; if the two figures fight, keep the stronger one alone.
- **Grade:** Partly supported [SRC-378, SRC-379].
- **Source:** Li et al. [SRC-378]; Ke et al. [SRC-379]; LOGO-8; `clever-marks.md`.
