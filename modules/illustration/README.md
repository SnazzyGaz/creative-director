# Module: illustration

Composition, focal point, shape, colour and value, edges, storytelling. Load with `core/`, and with `technical-drawing` when the picture depicts space or figures.

Format: **What / Why / Check / Fix / Source**. These are strong defaults; a deliberate exception is allowed when the brief supports it (record it in the critique).

---

### ILL-1. Composition: unequal divisions
- **What:** In multi-part scenes, divide the frame unequally: big, medium and small areas, with the horizon and main dividing lines away from the exact middle. Avoid tangents (edges that just touch).
- **Why:** Equal halves and kissing edges make a picture static or confusing. This rule is about *divisions*. For a single subject there is no default: centred and off-centre are equally valid, so judge placement by the brief's intent (maintainer decision, 2026-11-01).
- **Check:** Measure where the horizon and main lines fall. Look for tangents: two contours touching, or a contour touching the frame edge. Do the big areas differ clearly in size?
- **Fix:** Move a halving line to a clearly unequal position (no specific ratio is required). Overlap or separate tangent shapes decisively. Don't move a single subject on or off centre just to obey a rule; move it only if the placement works against the intent.
- **Source:** NN/g, *5 Principles of Visual Design* (balance) [S2]; Gurney, *Getting a Fresh Eye* [S7]; Bucci, big-medium-small [S79]; evidence on thirds and centring [S43, S44].

### ILL-2. One focal point
- **What:** One area holds the strongest value contrast, the sharpest edges, the most detail and (often) the most saturated colour.
- **Why:** Competing focal points split attention.
- **Check:** Blur test: what leads? List where contrast, edge, detail and saturation peak; are they in the same place?
- **Fix:** Concentrate the peaks; lower the others. Pyle's advice as Gurney relays it: light against light, dark against dark, and black against white only at the centre of interest.
- **Source:** Gurney, *How Many Values?* [S5]; NN/g squint test [S1].

### ILL-3. Shape: clear, varied silhouettes
- **What:** Main subjects read as a clear silhouette; shapes vary in size and character (not all the same blob).
- **Why:** At thumbnail size, silhouette is all that reads.
- **Check:** Fill the subject solid black on white. Is the action/object still clear? Are the big shapes varied?
- **Fix:** Turn the pose or object for a clearer outline; separate limbs from the body; vary shape sizes: one dominant shape, some medium, a few small. Alternate straights and curves along a contour rather than using all curves or all straights.
- **Source:** Gurney, *Linking Darks* (shape welding / notan) [S16]; Gurney, *Cure for Middle Value Mumbling* [S6]; Valve tested its *Team Fortress 2* characters as flat silhouettes, each class recognisable by shape alone [S83]; Bucci, shape design [S79]; Mattesi, straights against curves [S74].
- **Example pair:** Gurney describes Edward Simmons' *Old Woman Peeling an Orange*: the dark shawl is linked with the dark door and wall into one mass, so the head and hands stand out. The contrast case is a scene where each dark object is a separate island and nothing leads [S16].

### ILL-4. Colour and value: plan the structure, then the palette
- **What:** Plan 2-4 value groups (see CORE-2), then a limited gamut whose colours have roles.
- **Why:** Value carries the read; a restricted gamut gives unity and mood.
- **Check:** Greyscale and posterise to 3-4 levels. Then sample the palette: does it fit inside one gamut shape on a colour wheel, or is it everything everywhere?
- **Fix:** Re-group values (pick one of Loomis's tonal plans, see `notes.md`). Compress the shadows into a narrow dark range to keep more steps for the lights (Fowkes). Then use a gamut mask to pick a smaller set of colours.
- **Source:** Gurney, *How Many Values?* [S5]; Gurney, gamut masking posts [S11]; Loomis, *Creative Illustration*, via Gurney's summary [S76]; Fowkes on value grouping [S80].

### ILL-5. Edges: hard where you want attention, soft elsewhere
- **What:** Sharp edges at the focal point and form turns that matter; softer or lost edges in shadow, distance and at the edges of the frame.
- **Why:** Edges control depth and where the eye stops. All-hard looks cut out; all-soft looks spineless.
- **Check:** Mark the hardest edges. Are they near the focal point? Are background lines softer where they pass behind foreground forms?
- **Fix:** Soften or lose edges away from focus and in dim areas; sharpen at the focus.
- **Source:** Gurney, *Depth and Edges*, *Edge Induction*, *Transitioning Edges* [S17, S18, S19].
- **Example pair:** Gurney shows Bouguereau softening the far leg's contour where it crosses the near leg (depth reads) versus a diagram where all edges are equally sharp and the shapes sit on one flat plane [S17].

### ILL-6. Storytelling: one readable moment
- **What:** The picture shows one clear moment: who, doing what, where, and what it means. Every element supports it.
- **Why:** A viewer gives an illustration a second or two; ambiguity loses them.
- **Check:** Show the thumbnail and write the story in one sentence. Does it match the brief? What did the eye go to first (Gurney's "what strikes them first" test)?
- **Fix:** Cut props that don't support the moment; point gesture, gaze and light toward the story point.
- **Source:** Gurney, *Getting a Fresh Eye* (ask what strikes a viewer first) [S7]; NN/g heuristic #8 (minimalism applies to content too) [S4].

### ILL-7. Intensity follows the story (contrast and affinity)
- **What:** Difference (contrast) raises visual intensity; similarity (affinity) lowers it. This works across space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement and rhythm. Put the most contrast at the story's peak.
- **Why:** If everything is equally intense, nothing builds.
- **Check:** For a single image: is the strongest contrast of shape, tone and colour at the story point? For a set (storyboard, comic page, carousel), plot intensity per frame. Does it rise toward the key moment?
- **Fix:** Calm the build-up with affinity (similar shapes, close values, analogous hues), then break it at the peak.
- **Source:** Block, *The Visual Story* [S78].

### ILL-8. Shape and direction carry mood (default, not a blocker)
- **What:** Horizontals read as calm and stable, verticals as active, diagonals as movement or tension. Pointed shapes feel threatening and rounded shapes feel safe. Larger reads as stronger, and position high in the frame reads as freedom or power.
- **Why:** A mood that fights the brief undermines it. Only the curved-versus-sharp part has lab support [S42]. The rest are craft conventions, not laws.
- **Check:** Name the brief's mood. List the dominant directions and shape types. Do they agree with the mood?
- **Fix:** Tilt or round off the dominant shapes to suit the mood, keeping the subject.
- **Source:** Bang, *Picture This* [S77]; Bar & Neta [S42].

### ILL-9. Colour is relative to its neighbours
- **What:** Shadow colour, warm and cool notes, and "grey" are judged against their surroundings. Shadows are not always blue. Up-facing shadow planes take on sky colour, and down-facing ones can be warm from bounced light.
- **Why:** Formula colour (every shadow blue) looks generated, not observed.
- **Check:** Sample a shadow and its neighbouring light. Is the temperature shift consistent with the stated light sources (key, sky, bounce)? Does the same colour appear in two places but look different because of its ground?
- **Fix:** Set shadow temperature from the fill light, not a formula. Adjust a colour against its ground until it reads as intended.
- **Source:** Gurney, *Color and Light* [S51]; Albers, *Interaction of Color* [S47].

### ILL-10. Gesture before structure (default, not a blocker)
- **What:** A figure starts from a few lines of action and rhythm (direction, balance, the main forces). Structure and anatomy are built on that gesture, not the reverse.
- **Why:** Figures built part by part come out stiff, even when every part is correct. Correct proportions don't rescue a pose with no direction.
- **Check:** Trace the main line of action from head to the weight-bearing foot. Is there one? Do the contours alternate between straights and curves, or are both sides of the limbs symmetrical curves?
- **Fix:** Push the line of action (more curve, or a clear diagonal), then rebuild the masses along it. Pair a straight side against a curved side on limbs.
- **Source:** Hampton, *Figure Drawing: Design and Invention* (gesture, then structure, then anatomy) [S73]; Mattesi, *Force* (straights against curves, rhythm) [S74].
- **Note:** formerly TD-CON-2 (moved 2026-11-01).

### ILL-11. Avoid the generic AI image look
- **What:** Don't let the generator's defaults make the decisions. Common tells: contrast and detail everywhere at once, a glaring sunset or golden-hour sky, HDR glow and rim light on every edge, saturated orange-teal grading, and an "epic cinematic" finish on any subject. The image counterpart of UI-7.
- **Why:** None is wrong alone. Together they flatten hierarchy (CORE-3, ILL-2), raise visual complexity, which predicts lower first-impression appeal [S35], and make the image read as unauthored [S108, S110].
- **Check:** Count the tells. Two or more: flag it. In `tools/look.py`, does the blur grid show contrast spread evenly, or peaking away from the focal point? Which light and palette choices come from the brief, and which are defaults?
- **Fix:** Name one light source and where it is. Name a medium (gouache, 35mm still, pencil) instead of "cinematic" or "epic". Cut mood adjectives. Quiet the sky or background, and keep the peak contrast at the focal point [S109]. Light and palette concept belong to the brief (CD-2), so ask before changing them.
- **Source:** practitioner write-ups, not research [S108, S109, S110]; complexity evidence [S35]. Found in the knight stress test (`examples/illustration-knight/`).

### ILL-12. Real subjects, especially food, must be physically plausible (MUST-BE-RIGHT for food)
- **What:** Imagery of something real (food, materials, products, plants) is built from what the subject is (FP-9), and every part must look like the real thing up close. Food is the strictest case: people judge whether it looks edible before anything else.
- **Why:** Generators fill large areas with repeated texture. At full size that reads as a mesh, waffle or tiled pattern instead of the real material, and on food it turns appetising into off-putting. A layout can't rescue an image that looks wrong to eat.
- **Check:** Crop the subject at 2-4× in at least three places and write one line per crop:
  1. **Structure:** do the parts behave like the real thing? Strands, grains, leaves and fibres separate, overlap, curl and end; they don't fuse into a grid or a lattice.
  2. **No tiling or mesh:** no repeated motif, waffle or net pattern, no identical clumps side by side.
  3. **Each component is right:** count and inspect the named parts (an egg has one yolk, set or runny white with crisp edges; crackers or chips are separate pieces with their own edges and bubbles; sauce sits on and between things, it doesn't print onto them).
  4. **Material and light:** gloss where wet or oily, matt where dry; steam or shine follows one light (TD-LIT-1).
  5. **No invented marks** on packaging, plates or garnish (BRAND-8).
- **Fix:** Regenerate from a subject sheet and real references (FP-9), describing structure ("separate, glossy, curling strands with visible ends") rather than the old picture. Try several candidates and pick the one that passes every crop. Never fix mesh by sharpening or adding grain.
- **Source:** FP-9; ILL-11; BRAND-8; TD-LIT-1.

## Myths and weak claims
- **Rule of thirds as law:** weak evidence; one study even found centring preferred for single subjects [S43, S44]. Neither is a default here (ILL-1); ILL-1 is about unequal *divisions*, not third points.
- **Golden-ratio spirals:** no reliable preference effect [S38, S39].
- **"Shadows are blue, lights are warm":** only under one kind of light. See ILL-9 [S51].
- **Colour-emotion tables** (red = anger): weakly supported [S46]. Take mood from the brief.

Depth (Itten's seven contrasts, McCloud on simplification, Loomis's tonal plans): `notes.md`.
