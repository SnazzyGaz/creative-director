# Module: writing (prose, web and email copy)

Load with `core/` when the job is words: website copy, email replies, newsletters, blurbs, about text, UI microcopy, empty/error states and supporting copy; also when a visual piece still needs copy drafted. This is on demand, not always-on. British English is the default unless the brief says otherwise.

**Hard failures:** changed locked copy, invented facts or claims, and buttons or errors that mislead. Stop delivery until corrected; missing facts can remain clearly marked questions in a working draft, never plausible filler in finished copy. Everything else is an advisory flag: name the fault, its effect and any reasoned exception. No dash ban, forbidden-word list, sentence-length quota or detector score.

This is a companion, not a replacement: CD-7 still checks fixed strings and counts on the render; AES-10 still forbids extra text and claims outside the brief. FP-3, UI-11 and BRAND-8 keep their existing fact, screen and mark constraints. Permission to draft words does not permit new facts, promises or slogans. Typography stays TYPE; placement stays LAY. Add the medium's modules when needed, not a visual-only critique in place of reading the words.

Format: **What / Why / Check / Fix / Grade / Source**. House guides are production practice, not scientific proof. Grades follow `core/evidence.md`; the procedures below are editorial methods, not experiments validating this checklist. Novel-writing instruction, wholesale imitation of a newsroom guide and legal counsel are out of scope.

---

### WRITE-1. Brief the reader and the job before polishing
- **What:** Name the reader, purpose, channel, actual next action (or none), tone/genre and English variety. Record what the reader knows, needs and may be feeling. Use the brief's existing fields and constraints; do not invent a persona or a need.
- **Why:** Content planned around visitor questions serves a different job from an organisation talking about itself [SRC-458, SRC-459]. Business tone depends on audience and purpose [SRC-460]; user-centric language explains reader-relevant meaning, not just internal feature names [SRC-451].
- **Check:** Can you state who this is for and what they should understand or do? Is this a reply, a newsletter, an about page or a sales page? Does the tone fit this moment rather than a generic friendly template?
- **Fix:** Write a short working brief. Ask only for missing facts that affect the result; label provisional assumptions. Carry the named tone into word choice and rhythm without manufacturing familiarity.
- **Grade:** Procedure; supporting advice is Craft wisdom and production usability guidance, not a universal tone experiment.
- **Source:** Redish, author page and authorised chapter 7 sample (opening/content-planning pages only) [SRC-458, SRC-459]; Purdue OWL [SRC-460]; NN/g [SRC-451].

### WRITE-2. Separate locked copy from draftable copy
- **What:** List exact locked strings, supplied facts/meaning and the parts you may write. Preserve facts without treating every supplied sentence as locked verbatim. Do not invent slogans, commitments, evidence or quotations. When exact wording conflicts with correctness, flag it rather than silently changing it.
- **Why:** Accuracy and source checking survive the move from newsroom practice to everyday copy [SRC-473]. Drafting authority is bounded by the brief; it does not cancel CD-7 or AES-10.
- **Check:** Compare names, dates, prices, quantities, qualifications and promises against the supplied material. Did a rewrite change certainty or turn an intention into a guarantee? Are unsupported draft placeholders unmistakable?
- **Fix:** Restore locked wording; undo factual drift; remove unauthorised additions. Ask the owner of the work to resolve a contradiction. Once draft copy is approved as fixed, check it on the final surface with CD-7.
- **Grade:** Procedure; accuracy is an explicit newsroom standard within its context, not evidence that one voice is better.
- **Source:** Reuters, public Standards and Values page, not the complete Handbook [SRC-473]; CD-7; AES-10; FP-3; UI-11; BRAND-8.

### WRITE-3. Earn every objective claim
- **What:** Trace every objective claim to supplied or verified evidence, including numbers, comparisons, benefits, testimonials and implied promises. Keep the necessary qualifications beside the claim. Missing information stays a question, not a plausible assertion.
- **Why:** UK CAP Code section 03 requires substantiation before publication for objective advertising claims and prohibits misleading impressions and material omissions [SRC-465]. This is a constraint within its scope, not legal advice or a rule for every jurisdiction. Historical advertising advice favours specific substantiated facts over vague superlatives [SRC-463].
- **Check:** For each claim, what supports it, when was it true and does the wording exceed the evidence? Do the headline and caveat contradict? Is opinion presented as fact? Do benefits displace specs a buyer needs [SRC-450]?
- **Fix:** Narrow the claim to what is supported, restore material limits or remove it. Request evidence rather than inventing a statistic, review, deadline, saving or guarantee. Flag uncertain regulatory scope for qualified review.
- **Grade:** Procedure for the claim audit; CAP is a published regulatory standard within scope. Hopkins is Craft wisdom, not modern conversion evidence.
- **Source:** ASA/CAP [SRC-465]; Hopkins, public-domain Being Specific chapter [SRC-463]; NN/g product-page guidance [SRC-450].

### WRITE-4. Give each part a job and put it in order
- **What:** Make the headline's promise relevant and honest; give each paragraph one main purpose. Order the reader's questions, answer the important one early and state the real next step. Supporting or “filler” copy must orient, explain, reassure truthfully or enable action; space need not be filled.
- **Why:** NN/g's 1997 studies support concise, scannable, objective web copy in their tested travel-site context [SRC-447, SRC-448], not a universal conversion uplift or a demand that every text be short. Later scanning and about-page work supports meaningful headings and layered answers [SRC-449, SRC-452]. Hopkins treats a headline as a way to reach the relevant reader [SRC-462].
- **Check:** Read headings alone; do they outline a useful, accurate answer? Reverse-outline paragraph jobs. In a reply, is the answer buried beneath ceremony? On an about page, can a reader tell what the organisation actually does? In a newsletter, do subject and preheader add useful information?
- **Fix:** Reorder before adding transition words. Cut repetition and introductions that carry no meaning; keep detail needed for a decision. Replies can answer, acknowledge the specific concern, give a genuine next step and stop; warmth is optional, not forbidden.
- **Grade:** Procedure and Craft wisdom; historical and later usability studies offer scoped support, not a proof of this exact sequence.
- **Source:** NN/g [SRC-447 to SRC-452]; Hopkins, Headlines [SRC-462]; Redish sample [SRC-459]; Purdue sample emails [SRC-461]; Mailchimp newsletters [SRC-455]; UNC Transitions [SRC-441].

### WRITE-5. Make the meaning concrete, not merely shorter
- **What:** Prefer concrete nouns and verbs; explain necessary specialist terms and keep related words, especially subject and verb, close enough to follow. Active voice is a useful default, not a ban on passive: passive may keep the right subject in focus or avoid inventing an unknown actor.
- **Why:** Strunk and Gopen/Swan offer principles for unity, concrete language and reader expectations, not sentence-length laws [SRC-435, SRC-439]. Clear-language production guidance allows useful passive and audience-specific terminology [SRC-445].
- **Check:** What happened, who acts and to what? Does an abstract noun hide the action? Can the reader find the subject and its verb? Is a longer explanation necessary, or only padded? Does a precise term serve the audience?
- **Fix:** Name the action and object; unpack only the term that needs it. Retain a useful technical term consistently. Use a scene or sensory detail when experience matters, direct telling or summary when it serves the task. The thought-verbs exercise can build a scene, but is not a law against thinking, summary or interiority [SRC-442, SRC-443].
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom. Universal “show, never tell” or thought-verb bans are Contested; the cited exercise and response disagree on scope.
- **Source:** Strunk, original pre-White text [SRC-435]; Gopen/Swan permission-labelled reprint [SRC-439]; GOV.UK [SRC-445]; Palahniuk and August [SRC-442, SRC-443]; Economist Education authorised jargon extract, not the full book [SRC-475].

### WRITE-6. Make the flow audible and the voice particular
- **What:** As a default, connect known information to new information. Give pronouns clear referents and transitions a real logical relationship. Keep the voice recognisable while tone fits the reader's situation. Read aloud for pace, emphasis and unintended repetition; do not flatten every job into one smooth register.
- **Why:** Gopen/Swan and UNC describe how context and sentence links help readers follow thought [SRC-439 to SRC-441]. Le Guin treats sound and rhythm as part of meaning [SRC-437, SRC-438]. Production guides distinguish stable voice from changing tone; their own brand voices are not templates for everybody [SRC-446, SRC-453, SRC-456].
- **Check:** Does each sentence attach to the previous thought? Are all paragraphs built to one rhythm regardless of purpose? Does a sympathetic reply sound cheerful about bad news? Do fragments, repetition or a sudden long sentence carry deliberate emphasis?
- **Fix:** Repair the order or referent before inserting “moreover”. Vary syntax where meaning needs a change of pace; retain useful repetition and deliberate fragments. Choose words this speaker could use in this situation, not arbitrary quirks or forced humour.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom; voice/tone guides illustrate production choices, not scientific proof of a universal human voice.
- **Source:** Gopen/Swan and UNC [SRC-439 to SRC-441]; Le Guin author-site session and authorised Steering the Craft extract [SRC-437, SRC-438]; GOV.UK tone, Mailchimp voice/tone and Microsoft brand voice [SRC-446, SRC-453, SRC-456]; Quiller-Couch, public-domain lectures, period prejudices excluded [SRC-436].

### WRITE-7. Diagnose cliché and fog instead of banning words
- **What:** Flag a dead metaphor, corporate abstraction or familiar phrase only with a diagnosis: an empty claim, worn image, obscured action or avoidable haze. Familiar labels and idioms can help recognition; originality is not synonym-swapping.
- **Why:** Orwell warns that ready-made phrases can replace thought, but permits exceptions [SRC-434]. Guardian guidance resists cliché and forced variation; the Economist extract allows explained, useful jargon [SRC-472, SRC-475]. These are craft judgements, not a blacklist established by experiment.
- **Check:** What exact information does the phrase add? Could any competitor say it unchanged? Does the metaphor still carry the intended image? Would a novel synonym make an ordinary label harder to understand?
- **Fix:** Replace the empty assertion with a supported detail or a direct action; otherwise cut it. Keep a familiar phrase when its clarity, humour or character earns it. Never launder the same vague claim with fresher adjectives.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom; exceptions must answer the reader's need, not an originality score.
- **Source:** Orwell [SRC-434]; Guardian P page [SRC-472]; Economist Education extract [SRC-475]; GOV.UK clear language [SRC-445].

### WRITE-8. Review rhetorical habits, never infer authorship
- **What:** Look for clusters of empty contrast (“not X, but Y”), fake three-part lists, repeated “whether you're a…”, stacked “moreover”, vague significance and unearned warmth. Ask what each does here. These are editorial prompts, not features all validated as AI-specific. A word, em dash or double hyphen is not proof of authorship. Do not add mistakes to look human.
- **Why:** Academic corpus studies find aggregate vocabulary shifts, not the author of an individual text [SRC-477, SRC-478]. Pew's web study and methodology also describe limited samples and imperfect detectors [SRC-481, SRC-482]. Rhetorical commentary offers useful diagnoses, not prevalence experiments [SRC-483]; AP confirms that the em dash is valid punctuation [SRC-474].
- **Check:** Does the contrast actually distinguish two things? Are the three items distinct and necessary? Does warmth acknowledge anything specific? Are parallel paragraphs, bullets or signposts helping navigation or manufacturing importance? Would the same pattern recur through every page?
- **Fix:** Keep rhetorical devices with a real job; rewrite empty patterns into the actual point. Change punctuation for meaning and house style, not a detector. Report text faults only, never an AI-authorship verdict or a score.
- **Grade:** Procedure for editorial review. Corpus findings are scoped observational support, not validation of this checklist. Causal accounts are Contested; a preprint abstract is preliminary.
- **Source:** Kobak et al. [SRC-477]; Kousha/Thelwall [SRC-478]; Juzek/Ward, abstract only [SRC-479]; Czuma, preliminary preprint abstract only [SRC-480]; Pew chart/methodology [SRC-481, SRC-482]; Gorrie essays [SRC-483, SRC-484]; Goedecke, explicitly speculative hypotheses [SRC-485]; Wikipedia community field guide, not a detector [SRC-486]; AP free post [SRC-474].

### WRITE-9. Make small copy tell the truth about the state
- **What:** A button names the action it really performs. An error names the problem and a remedy the reader can actually use; do not present service failure as bad user input. Empty-state copy matches the actual state, with no call to action when nothing can be done.
- **Why:** GOV.UK's button and error patterns connect text to real consequences and recovery [SRC-466, SRC-467]. GitLab and Mozilla distinguish empty, search and onboarding contexts; title, body and action are not all mandatory [SRC-468, SRC-469].
- **Check:** Click through or verify the documented behaviour: does “Save” save, and is a supposed fix possible? Does an empty state falsely say there are no records while data is loading? Is a link descriptive in context? Does cheerfulness blame or trivialise a problem?
- **Fix:** Correct the wording or flag the behaviour; never promise functionality to make the copy neater. State the known problem and available next step. Remove repeated titles and decorative CTAs. Apply UX and UI accessibility/state rules too; words alone do not certify the interface.
- **Grade:** Procedure, grounded in published production patterns; not a claim that one wording always wins.
- **Source:** GOV.UK Design System [SRC-466, SRC-467]; GitLab Pajamas [SRC-468]; Mozilla Acorn [SRC-469].

### WRITE-10. Set a house style without making it grammar law
- **What:** Default to British English unless briefed otherwise. Choose consistent numbers, titles, quotation marks, heading case, serial-comma policy and dash treatment. Preserve official names, quoted spelling and meaning. Distinguish hyphen, en dash, em dash and minus; do not use `--` as an accidental typography substitute.
- **Why:** Newsroom, product and community guides make different legitimate choices [SRC-444, SRC-454, SRC-470, SRC-471, SRC-476]. AP uses spaced em dashes; Microsoft and Mailchimp use unspaced ones [SRC-474, SRC-457, SRC-454]. None establishes a dash ban or a universal contraction policy.
- **Check:** Is a difference required by an official name or quotation, or accidental? Does comma placement change the meaning? Is a dash marking a useful interruption, or would a comma, colon, stop or parenthesis express the relationship better? Are ranges and negative numbers unambiguous?
- **Fix:** Record the choices in the brief's constraints or a short local style sheet, then apply them consistently. Recast ambiguity instead of fighting over a serial comma. Keep clarity exceptions; do not import historical grammar prejudices, a newsroom's contraction ban or a product guide's tone wholesale.
- **Grade:** Procedure; house-style selections are Craft wisdom, not scientific rules of English.
- **Source:** GOV.UK A–Z [SRC-444]; Mailchimp mechanics [SRC-454]; BBC grammar/spelling/punctuation [SRC-470]; Guardian O [SRC-471]; Wikipedia Manual of Style [SRC-476]; Microsoft dashes [SRC-457]; AP official free em-dash post, 27 August 2025 [SRC-474].

### WRITE-11. Finish on the surface where it will be read
- **What:** Check facts against the brief and evidence, scan headings alone, read aloud, then inspect the delivered context: subject/preheader, narrow screen, truncation, button width, links and surrounding copy. A prose review is not permission to send or publish.
- **Why:** Layer-cake scanning work supports accurate descriptive headings [SRC-449]; newsletter production guidance calls for meaningful subjects, testing and read-aloud [SRC-455]. The Ogilvy memo reproduction advocates revision and a clear intended action, but is a secondary source, not a tested universal length rule [SRC-464].
- **Check:** Can the intended reader find the answer and next step without reading every word? Did trimming lose a qualification? Does a truncated subject mislead? Does the approved wording survive wrapping/rendering (CD-7)? Are unresolved questions visibly marked rather than shipped as facts?
- **Fix:** Repair the failure in context, then recheck facts, flow and the changed region. Use a second reader or task test when stakes warrant it. Deliver the copy with any unresolved hard failure named; never certify missing evidence as a pass.
- **Grade:** Procedure; revision advice is Craft wisdom and production practice, not validation of this entire pass.
- **Source:** NN/g Layer-Cake [SRC-449]; Mailchimp newsletters [SRC-455]; Ogilvy's 1982 memo via The Marginalian, secondary reproduction [SRC-464]; Le Guin [SRC-437, SRC-438]; Reuters [SRC-473]; CD-7.
