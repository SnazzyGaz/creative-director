# First-principles remake: step back, then rebuild

**Purpose:** the critique loop is a light touch. It keeps the layout idea and nudges the craft. Sometimes the owner of the work wants the opposite: "redo this from scratch", "rejig it", "start over", "reimagine it". Polishing the old layout doesn't answer that ask. This mode steps back first: what the piece is, who it is for and what it has to do. Then it rebuilds from that, using the same rules and checks as everything else.

Grade: every FP rule is a Procedure (`core/evidence.md`); the rules it calls on keep their own grades.

Load with: `core/principles.md`, `core/creative-direction.md`, `core/critique-loop.md`, the medium's module, and `modules/aesthetic-critique/`. The brief block to fill in is at the end of this file; the rest of the critique uses `core/critique-template.md`.

```
step back (FP-2) -> check the facts (FP-3) -> brief, signed off (FP-4) -> 2-3 concepts (FP-5)
   -> recommend one, owner picks (FP-6) -> imagery from the subject (FP-9) -> build -> normal critique loop and full QA (FP-7)
```

---

## FP-1. Use this mode only when asked
- **What:** The triggers are "from scratch", "redo", "rejig", "start over", "reimagine" and "rethink", or an explicit "don't just polish it". Without one of these, the light touch stays the default (`critique-loop.md` section 5). A take-over (section 6) is not this mode: a take-over repairs a broken piece and keeps its idea. This mode questions the idea itself.
- **Why:** Reframing is how designers handle a problem the first framing can't solve [SRC-164, SRC-165]. But redoing work that only needed a nudge throws away what the owner made and approved (CD-5, AES-10).
- **Check:** Can you quote the words that put you in this mode? If you can't, use the light touch, or ask.

## FP-2. Step back: answer the seven questions before you draw anything
- **What:** Write one line for each:
  1. **What it is:** the type of piece, the medium, and where and how it is seen (size, distance, time spent on it). Design for that real medium and use: a printed poster, a feed post, a board read from the pavement. Don't design for incidental framing, such as how a camera roll, screenshot or chat preview happened to crop the reference. Don't infer constraints from how a reference image was captured (its crop, angle, glare or resolution).
  2. **Who it is for:** the people who use it, and what they already know.
  3. **Its one job:** what someone should do, know or feel afterwards, in one sentence.
  4. **The feeling:** the tone it should carry, in three words or fewer.
  5. **The given facts:** the text, data, names, dates, logos and constraints that were supplied, listed word for word.
  6. **What must survive:** the facts, the official marks (FP-8), anything the owner has asked to keep, and any constraint in the brief. Separately, list what was incidental in the old version: its layout, styling, extra copy and default choices.
  7. **Success:** how you'll judge it done, tied to its use (e.g. "the date reads from 3 m").
  Mark each line [brief] or [inferred], as in AES-0.
- **Why:** Rand describes the designer as starting from careful study rather than a ready-made idea. They break the given material down into its parts and restate the problem, which can mean discarding or revising much of what they were given [SRC-163]. Schön calls this problem setting: naming what to attend to and framing the context [SRC-164]. The d.school's "define" step turns findings into a short point of view that later ideas are judged against [SRC-166].
- **Check:** Is every line filled, with no line describing the old layout? Are lines 3 and 7 specific enough to rule a concept out? Does line 1 name the real medium, not the way the reference was photographed or cropped?

## FP-3. Check the given facts against each other; never invent
- **What:** First transcribe every fact from the original, word for word, before designing anything: names, items, prices, dates, times, contact details, and small print such as "image for reference only" or terms. Then read them as data. Check that dates match weekdays, sums add up, names are spelled the same each time, and URLs and prices agree. Flag claims that contradict each other (an item labelled both "new" and "best seller", a weekday that doesn't match its date) and claims with no source ("No. 1", "award-winning") as owner questions. Disclaimers and legal lines are given facts: keep them. A conflict is a question for the owner. Until it is answered, set only what is not in doubt, and say what you held back. A remake adds no new claims, prices, quotes, characters or slogans (CD-1, AES-10).
- **Why:** Rand notes that the given material is often inadequate and that the designer's job includes restating it [SRC-163]. Restating it doesn't make inventing it acceptable. An error in a fact costs the owner more than any styling.
- **Check:** Is there a transcription of the original, then one line per fact checked, with conflicts and unsourced claims listed as questions? Did every disclaimer survive? Does the remake contain any word or claim that is not in the given facts?

## FP-4. Write the brief from first principles and get it signed off
- **What:** Condense FP-2 and FP-3 into 5-8 lines: what it is and where it is seen; who it is for; the one job; the feeling; what must survive; what is free to change; how it will be judged; open questions. Send it as the first message, and send it before any concepts (FLOW-1). "Just fix it" or "just do it" skips the wait but not the brief: state it at the top of the delivery (FLOW-4). Name it up front as a remake, not a polish (FLOW-5).
- **Why:** A brief works as a platform that opens ideas up, and it is the agreed yardstick for "done" [SRC-88, SRC-89, SRC-90]. IDEO frames a challenge as one short, memorable problem statement that still allows many solutions. Its quick test: if you can think of five possible answers, the frame is about right [SRC-167]. A point of view gives a reference for judging competing ideas, and it gets revised as you learn [SRC-166].
- **Check:** Is it 5-8 lines? Could a stranger make the piece from it without seeing the old version? Has the owner signed it off, or is it stated at the top of the delivery?

## FP-5. Make 2-3 concepts that differ in structure, not only in style
- **What:** From the brief, not from the old layout, make 2-3 rough concepts. Each gets a one-line idea, a thumbnail, and a note of what leads in it. They must differ in structure: what leads, how the space is divided, and the reading order or the metaphor. A different typeface or palette on the same layout is a variation, not a concept. Make at least one concept that the old version could not have become by polishing.
- **Why:** Making designs in parallel gives better results and more variety than refining one at a time [SRC-95]. Frame creation means trying new framings of the same need, not new surfaces on the old frame [SRC-165]. Process models across fields separate analysis (what is) from synthesis (what could be) [SRC-168]. Dubberly also notes that a proposed solution often redefines the problem, so let a concept send you back to the brief [SRC-169].
- **Check:** The structure test: describe each concept in one sentence without naming a font, colour or effect. Are the sentences different? Does each one answer the brief's one job?

## FP-6. Recommend one, with reasons; the owner chooses
- **What:** Score each concept against the brief's success line and its one job, not against taste. Recommend one. Give one line on why, and one line per dropped concept on what it does better and why it lost. The owner picks; then build only that one.
- **Why:** Choose against the brief and drop the rest early (CD-3). Being clear about what was traded makes the choice reviewable (CD-6, AES-9).
- **Check:** Does each reason cite a line of the brief? Is there a dropped-concept line for each of the others?

## FP-7. Build it, then run the full loop and every gate
- **What:** Build the chosen concept at its real size, then run the normal critique loop:
  - the five look tests;
  - the anatomy pass on every figure, hand, animal and prop (CHAR-9 to CHAR-11), or "n/a" with an inventory showing there are none;
  - one key light direction (TD-LIT-1), or "n/a: flat, no modelled light";
  - type size and contrast (TYPE-3, UI-6);
  - marks (BRAND-4, BRAND-8);
  - additions (AES-10).
  Then compare side by side with both the original and a light-touch fix, if one exists (AES-9): which is stronger for the brief, why, and at what cost. "The light touch was enough" is an allowed answer. Report it and offer the polish.
- **Why:** A remake drops the light-touch cap, not the checks. The MUST-BE-RIGHT list still blocks (`critique-loop.md`), and regenerating is exactly where subjects drift and hands break (section 6).
- **Check:** Is there a delivery checklist (FLOW-3) with the brief at the top, the three-way comparison, and the QA line?

## FP-8. Official marks survive the remake unaltered
- **What:** Logos, crests, team badges, sponsor marks and the client's own mark are given facts, not styling. They stay in the remake at their official proportions and colours, sourced and placed as in BRAND-4. They are never redrawn, restyled or generated. When a remake uses generated imagery, check it for invented marks (BRAND-8).
- **Why:** A mark identifies who something comes from. Changing it changes what the piece claims [SRC-170, SRC-171], and brand guides forbid altering one [SRC-172, SRC-173].
- **Check:** BRAND-4 and BRAND-8 pass on the final render.

## FP-9. Build imagery from what the subject really is, not by re-describing the reference
- **What:** When a remake needs new imagery (generated, photographed or drawn), start from the subject, not from the old picture. Write down what the subject physically is: its parts, materials, textures, how it is made or served, and how it behaves in light. Gather real references of the subject (photos with known sources), then art-direct: the angle, light, crop and the space the layout needs. Never prompt by describing the old image ("a bowl like the one in this picture"); that carries its artefacts, odd crops and errors into the new one. For food and other real things, run a plausibility check at 2-4× crops (ILL-12) before the image goes into the layout.
- **Why:** A reference shows one capture of the subject, with its defects. Rebuilding from the subject is the same move as rebuilding the layout from the brief rather than the old layout [SRC-163, SRC-165]. Image generators reproduce what they are told, including the mistakes in a description of a flawed image.
- **Check:** Is there a subject sheet (what it is, its parts, 2+ real references with sources) and an art-direction line? Does the prompt or shot list describe the subject and the direction, not the old image? Did the ILL-12 crops pass?
- **Fix:** Rewrite the prompt or shot list from the subject sheet; regenerate or reshoot; reject any candidate that fails ILL-12, however good the composition.

## What this mode is not
- It is not a licence to change the facts, the owner's marks or anything the owner has asked to keep.
- It is not a style swap. The structure test in FP-5 catches that.
- It is not the default. Light-touch fixes remain the default for every other ask.

## Template: brief from first principles

Send this first, then wait.

```markdown
- Trigger (FP-1): <the owner's words>
- Step back (FP-2), one line each, [brief] or [inferred]: what it is and where it is seen / who it is for / its one job / the feeling / the given facts, word for word / must survive vs incidental / success
- Facts (FP-3): <transcription of the original, word for word, incl. disclaimers> then <one line per check; contradictions and unsourced claims as questions; what is held back until answered>
- Imagery (FP-9, ILL-12; only if new imagery is made): <subject sheet: what it is, its parts, 2+ real references with sources> · <art direction> · <2-4× crops: structure / no tiling / components / material and light / marks>
- **Brief (5-8 lines, FP-4):** <what and where> · <for whom> · <one job> · <feeling> · <must survive, incl. official marks (FP-8)> · <free to change> · <judged done when> · <open questions>
- Concepts (FP-5), each with a thumbnail: A <one-line idea; what leads> / B <...> / C <...>. Structure test: <the one-sentence descriptions differ without naming a font, colour or effect: yes / no>
- Recommend (FP-6): <concept, reason citing a brief line>. Dropped: <one line each: what it does better, why it lost>. The owner picks.
- Comparison after the build (FP-7, AES-9): original vs light-touch fix (if any) vs remake: <stronger for the brief, why, cost>
```
