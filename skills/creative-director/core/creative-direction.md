# Creative direction: brief first, then craft

**Purpose:** the critique loop checks craft. This file adds the step a creative director takes before that: does the work answer the brief, for its audience? A bot acts as its own first-pass director. The yardstick is the brief, not taste. Where the brief is silent, the question goes to the owner of the work (the person who commissioned it).

Keep it light. These are defaults, not blockers; only the MUST-BE-RIGHT list in `critique-loop.md` blocks. Grade: every CD rule is a Procedure, built on the practice and studies cited (`evidence.md`).

## CD-1. Have a brief before making anything
- **What:** Six short lines: the problem or purpose; the audience; the one idea (a single sentence); where and how big it is seen; must-keeps and constraints (brand, subject, format); how you'll judge it done.
- **Why:** Weak briefs waste work. In a 2022 survey most agency staff called client briefs inadequate, and few marketers had agreed criteria for judging the work [S88]. A good brief is a platform that opens up ideas, not a box that closes them [S89, S90, S107].
- **Check:** Can you fill all six lines? If not, ask the owner, or write your assumptions down and flag them.
- **Read it exactly:** count what the brief fixes ("it" means one; a named number is that number), and keep background facts as mood rather than forcing each into the picture or the copy (ILL-6).
- **Production cost:** note how it will be made and paid for. Full-colour print, 3D renders or special finishes may be wasteful on a council or charity budget; ask, or offer a cheaper version that keeps the idea (owner's note on a batch 4 council notice, S44).

## CD-2. Set the direction once, then hold it
- **What:** Before styling, write 3-5 mood words or "pillars", a do/don't list, and the tokens that follow from them (palette roles, type, shape, light). Use your own or generated references; describe, don't copy.
- **Why:** Mood boards and art guides frame the task, align people and steer later work [S97, S98]. Game art directors turn pillars into a guide with do/don't examples and review against it [S99, S100]. Film production designers do the same for a whole production [S106]. Without this, defaults decide (UI-7).
- **Check:** Does each colour, type and layout choice trace back to the direction? If it traces to a default, flag it.

## CD-3. Try a few options, choose, and drop the rest early
- **What:** For an open brief, make 2-3 clearly different rough options (thumbnails, quick renders) before polishing one. Pick against the brief and give a one-line reason for each option you drop.
- **Why:** In a controlled study, making designs in parallel gave better results and more variety than refining one at a time [S95]. Diverge, then converge [S96]. Rough work keeps attention on the idea, not the finish [S91]. Being wrong early is cheap [S94]. Go for one strong idea [S92].
- **Check:** Were options compared before polishing? Is the chosen one the best answer to CD-1, not just the first one made?
- **Concept fit:** test each option against the product's category and senses, not only its name: a coffee named after a harbour still has to look like something to drink, not a cleaning product. Look for readings nobody meant (a lone object can suggest an accident). An event sold on fun shows the activity and the people. Details: EN-2 items 7 and 8 (`energy-pass.md`).

## CD-4. Feedback says what and why, not how
- **What:** Name the problem, where it is, and why it matters for the brief or audience. A fix is offered as an example; the maker decides how to solve it. Talk about the work, never the maker.
- **Why:** Pixar's Braintrust gives notes without authority to mandate fixes, because the cause of a problem often lies elsewhere [S94]. Feedback that pulls attention to the person rather than the task often lowers performance [S102]. Specific, actionable, justified critique is rated more helpful [S103]. Annotated, precise notes suit later stages, once the concept is locked [S104].
- **Check:** This is how the template's Fix lines are already written. Keep fixes small and tied to a rule ID.

## CD-5. Protect the idea through every round
- **What:** Each fix, and especially a take-over rework, keeps the one idea and the must-keeps from CD-1. After each round, re-read the brief.
- **Why:** Directors guard the core idea against compromise [S87, S89, S93]. In the stress tests, a rework fixed the perspective but quietly dropped part of the subject (`examples/technical-drawing-still-life/`).
- **Check:** Is anything in the brief's must-keeps missing or changed? If so, flag it as CD-5, ranked first.

## CD-6. Done means the brief is met
- **What:** Stop when the work meets the CD-1 criteria with no blockers. If the next change serves taste rather than the brief or audience, it's done.
- **Why:** A key art-direction skill is telling real improvement from personal preference [S101]. Agreed criteria make "done" checkable [S88]. The loop's stop rules apply (`critique-loop.md` section 7).

## When the ask is "from scratch"
CD-1 to CD-6 assume the brief is given. When the owner asks for a redo, rejig or reimagining, write the brief yourself from first principles, get it signed off, and compare 2-3 structurally different concepts before building: `core/first-principles.md` (FP-1..9).

## Presenting the result
Lead with the brief and the idea. Show the chosen option, a one-line reason, and what was dropped and why. Answer the concerns people raise rather than selling harder [S105]. Report open questions plainly.
