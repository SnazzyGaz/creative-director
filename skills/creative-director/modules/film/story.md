# Film: story first

The core of the film module. Technique is judged by what it does for the story beat: what the viewer should feel, know or wonder at that moment. A technically clean shot that serves no beat is a weaker shot than a rough one that lands it. Read this before `README.md`; the craft rules there are tools for the rules here.

Format: **What / Why / Check / Fix / Grade / Source**. Evidence is thinner here than for perception: most of these are craft wisdom, and the research that exists (music, shot scale, colour associations) shows tendencies that depend on context. Say "craft default" unless the grade says otherwise. Taste calls go to the owner of the work (AES-12).

---

### FILM-STORY-0. Name the beat, then check every choice against it (the beat check)
- **What:** Before judging a shot, scene or clip, write its beat in one line: what changes for the character or the viewer, and what the viewer should feel or know by the end. Then ask of each choice: shot size, angle, lens and distance, focus, light, colour, movement, cut point and length, sound, music, text. Does it serve this beat, fight it, or do nothing? This is AES-0 for moving images.
- **Why:** Defaults (eye level, medium shot, slow push-in, music everywhere, teal and orange) are what make clips generic. The beat is what turns a default into a choice.
- **Check:** Fill the table below. Any "default" or "fights it" with no reason is a question for the owner of the work or a fix.

| Choice | What it does here | Serves / neutral / fights the beat |
|---|---|---|
| Shot size and distance | | |
| Angle and height | | |
| Lens, focus, depth | | |
| Light (key, ratio, source) | | |
| Colour (palette, change) | | |
| Camera movement | | |
| Staging and blocking | | |
| Cut point, shot length, rhythm | | |
| Sound, silence, music | | |
| On-screen text | | |

- **Fix:** Change what fights the beat; turn defaults into choices or remove them (CORE-8).
- **Grade:** Procedure.
- **Source:** [SRC-78, SRC-270, SRC-276]; AES-0.

### FILM-STORY-1. Show, don't tell
- **What:** Carry story information by image, action and sound before dialogue, titles or voice-over. If a line explains what the picture could show (or already shows), cut the line or change the picture.
- **Why:** Viewers trust and remember what they infer more than what they are told, and doubled information slows the scene.
- **Check:** Mute the sound: does the beat still read? Read the dialogue alone: does it repeat the picture?
- **Fix:** Replace an explaining line with a visible action or a reaction; drop redundant titles.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-276, SRC-78, SRC-270].
- **Source:** [SRC-276, SRC-78, SRC-270].

### FILM-STORY-2. Shot size and framing set emotional distance and power
- **What:** Closer means more access to a character's mind and feeling; wider means context, scale or isolation. Frame position, height and angle shift power: who is higher, who is larger, who has more space. A frame within a frame (doorway, window, mirror) can trap or watch a character; wide negative space can isolate them. Keep the closest shot of a scene for its most important moment.
- **Why:** In one experiment, viewers of a close-up of a sad character talked more about mental states afterwards (a shift in tendency, not in ability); angle effects on judgement exist but are small and conditional.
- **Check:** Plot shot size against the scene's beats: does the closest shot fall on the turn? Does framing show the power relation the scene is about, and does it shift when power shifts?
- **Fix:** Re-cover the scene so size and framing follow the emotional arc; let power changes show in frame position and height.
- **Grade:** Partly supported for closeness and mental-state attention [SRC-289]; angle and power: Partly supported, conditional [SRC-290, SRC-291]; frame-within-frame and negative space: craft wisdom [SRC-78, SRC-267, SRC-271].
- **Source:** [SRC-289, SRC-290, SRC-291, SRC-78, SRC-267, SRC-271]; FILM-SHOT-1, FILM-SHOT-2, ILL-15, ILL-16.

### FILM-STORY-3. Colour is planned as an arc (the colour script)
- **What:** Lay out one small frame or palette per scene in story order (a colour script). Tie palettes to characters, places or states; shift them at turning points; bring a palette back to recall what it meant.
- **Why:** Seen at a glance, a colour script shows whether colour follows the emotional arc or sits in one grade throughout. Colour-emotion associations are broadly shared across cultures (a common core with real differences by language and place), but they are weak and context-bound, so meaning comes mostly from how the film itself pairs a colour with a character or event.
- **Check:** Build the strip (one frame per scene) and read it without the film: can you see the turns? Does each palette change happen at a story change, and is it motivated by the light or the world?
- **Fix:** Re-plan the palette per section; grade or relight to the plan; take mood words from the brief, not from a colour-meaning table.
- **Grade:** Colour scripting: craft wisdom [SRC-306, SRC-305, SRC-328]; colour-emotion associations: Partly supported, culture- and context-dependent [SRC-304, SRC-46]. Fixed colour meanings are a myth (`edit.md`).
- **Source:** [SRC-306, SRC-305, SRC-328, SRC-304, SRC-46].

### FILM-STORY-4. Light sets mood, and a change of light is a beat
- **What:** Low key (deep shadow, high ratio) for threat, secrecy or intimacy; high key for openness or comedy; shadow to hide what the story withholds. When the light changes inside a scene (a lamp switched off, the sun going in, a door opening), it should land on a story change and have a cause in the world.
- **Why:** Light is read before anything is understood; a change of light the audience can see carries the turn without a word.
- **Check:** Does the key and ratio suit the beat? Is there a light change, and does it fall on a turn? Is it motivated?
- **Fix:** Re-light to the beat; time the change to the turn; give it a source (FILM-LIT-2).
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-273, SRC-268, SRC-274, SRC-323]. The finding that films have grown darker over decades is descriptive only [SRC-284].
- **Source:** [SRC-273, SRC-268, SRC-274, SRC-323, SRC-284].

### FILM-STORY-5. Visual intensity rises and falls with the story (contrast and affinity)
- **What:** Plan an intensity curve for the piece: calm beats use affinity (similar shapes, tones, colours, movement, rhythm), peaks use contrast in one or more of those components. The climax gets the strongest contrast; resolution returns to affinity.
- **Why:** A piece at one intensity has no peaks. Popular films also show measurable structure at climaxes, such as shorter shots and more motion.
- **Check:** Sketch the story curve, then plot visual intensity per scene (shot length, movement, contrast, colour). Do they rise and fall together?
- **Fix:** Lower intensity where the story is calm; save contrast for the peaks. Same principle as ILL-7.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-78]; structural changes at climaxes in popular films: Partly supported, descriptive [SRC-286, SRC-285].
- **Source:** [SRC-78, SRC-286, SRC-285]; ILL-7.

### FILM-STORY-6. Camera movement expresses subjectivity and tension
- **What:** Approaching the subject intensifies; pulling away releases or isolates; following puts us with a character; a point-of-view or close handheld camera makes us share their state; a locked-off frame observes. Speed tracks tension: a slow creep builds it, a sudden move releases or shocks.
- **Why:** Movement changes our relation to the subject, not just what we see.
- **Check:** For each move, which of these is it, and does it fit the beat? Does the scene's movement change when the scene's tension changes?
- **Fix:** Re-plan the movement to the beat; lock off where nothing should shift (FILM-CAM-1).
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-268, SRC-271]; narrow neuro evidence that a smooth approaching camera engages viewers' motor responses more [SRC-292].
- **Source:** [SRC-268, SRC-271, SRC-292].

### FILM-STORY-7. Music says what the beat means (or deliberately says the opposite)
- **What:** Choose score (outside the story world) or source music (inside it, heard by characters) on purpose. Music can match the scene's feeling (empathetic), play against it (anempathetic or ironic, such as a cheerful song over violence), carry a leitmotif (a theme tied to a character or idea that returns, changed, as they change), or bridge scenes. Cut to music's phrasing at key moments, not on every beat. Leave room without music.
- **Why:** Music measurably changes how viewers read ambiguous action, what they expect and remember, and how they relate to characters. Wall-to-wall music numbs, and music that contradicts the beat by accident misleads.
- **Check:** Mute and unmute each cue: what does the music add or change? Is the meaning it adds the one the beat wants? Is a counterpoint cue clearly deliberate? Do themes recur with their character or idea?
- **Fix:** Re-place, re-time or remove cues; give a recurring idea its own theme; edit to phrases.
- **Grade:** Well supported that music shapes interpretation, memory and attitude to characters [SRC-298, SRC-299, SRC-300, SRC-301]; the devices (leitmotif, counterpoint, bridges): craft wisdom [SRC-302, SRC-270, SRC-330].
- **Source:** [SRC-298, SRC-299, SRC-300, SRC-301, SRC-302, SRC-270, SRC-330].

### FILM-STORY-8. Silence and sound design carry feeling and space
- **What:** Silence (or near-silence) is a tool at a peak, by contrast with what came before. Off-screen sound extends space and builds suspense about what we can't see. A sound bridge carries one scene's sound into the next to link them. Sound perspective matches the shot (close sounds close).
- **Why:** Sound adds meaning viewers attribute to the picture ("added value"), and sound alone can shift how a face reads.
- **Check:** Where is the quietest moment, and does it fall on a beat? Is anything heard but not seen, and does it pay off? Do scene changes use bridges on purpose?
- **Fix:** Pull sound out before or at a peak; add motivated off-screen sound; bridge where scenes link in meaning.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom with a perceptual account [SRC-302, SRC-270]; sound shifting facial reads: Partly supported [SRC-297].
- **Source:** [SRC-302, SRC-270, SRC-297].

### FILM-STORY-9. Tension comes from what the audience knows (suspense, withholding, reveal)
- **What:** Suspense: let the audience know something a character doesn't (the bomb under the table) and make them wait. Surprise: withhold it and reveal it at once. Choose which the beat needs, and control the order and timing of information: what is shown, what is held back, what is revealed and when.
- **Why:** A short shock is spent at once; knowledge plus waiting holds the audience for the whole scene.
- **Check:** For each tense scene, write what the audience knows that the characters don't, and the reverse. Is the reveal timed on purpose? Is information given away too early or so late it can't be felt?
- **Fix:** Reorder shots or scenes; plant the information earlier for suspense; hold it for surprise.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom (one director's formulation, widely taught) [SRC-303].
- **Source:** [SRC-303, SRC-269, SRC-270].

### FILM-STORY-10. Reaction shots and point of view say whose scene it is
- **What:** Decide whose scene it is. Their coverage is closer, their eyeline and point-of-view shots lead (look, then what they see, then their reaction), and the cut often goes to the listener, not the speaker, at emotional moments.
- **Why:** The reaction tells the audience how to feel about what just happened; context also shifts how a face is read, within limits (FILM-EDIT-5).
- **Check:** Whose scene is it, and does coverage and cutting favour them? Is a key line played on the speaker when the listener's reaction is the beat?
- **Fix:** Re-cut to reactions; add or remove point-of-view shots; re-cover.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-271, SRC-270]; context effects on faces: Partly supported [SRC-295, SRC-296].
- **Source:** [SRC-271, SRC-270, SRC-295, SRC-296].

### FILM-STORY-11. Motifs and rhymes gather meaning
- **What:** A repeated image, composition, colour, object, movement or sound gathers meaning each time it returns. A visual rhyme (a composition or action echoed later, changed) shows how a character or situation has moved.
- **Why:** Repetition with change lets the film say "this is like that, and different" without a word.
- **Check:** List the motifs. Does each appear at least twice? Does it change or pay off? Is any repeat accidental and confusing?
- **Fix:** Plant the motif earlier, vary it at the turn, pay it off at the end, or cut the accidental repeat.
- **Grade:** Craft wisdom [SRC-270, SRC-78].
- **Source:** [SRC-270, SRC-78].
