# Clever marks: double readings, mashups and hidden figures

Reference for LOGO-8 and LOGO-16. Load when a mark combines two ideas in one shape. The general craft of representing an idea is in `mark-making.md`. Famous marks are cited examples only: describe them, never draw or reproduce them (BRAND-4, BRAND-8). Grades follow `core/evidence.md`.

## Techniques

| Technique | How it works | Cited examples (designer, year) |
|---|---|---|
| **Figure-ground reversal** | the space between or around the shapes makes a second figure | an arrow between E and x, Lindon Leader at Landor, 1994 [SRC-355]; a lion and a gorilla face to face in the gaps of a zoo's tree, designer uncredited [SRC-392]; a Spartan helmet formed by the space around a golfer's swing, Richard Fonteneau, 2010 [SRC-383] |
| **Shared contour** | one line belongs to two objects at once | a white swan whose neck wraps round a black mallard to form an ampersand, John Randall, 2014 [SRC-384]; a pen nib whose slit and gap form a spoon, 300million, 2005 [SRC-386] |
| **Letter plus object** | letters double as a picture | a W and a whale tail with an H in the gap, Peter Good, 1979 [SRC-382]; a rider made of the letters of "Tour", a yellow disc as wheel and jersey, Joël Guenoun, 2002 [SRC-385]; m and b as a glove holding a ball, Tom Meindel, 1977 [SRC-389]; flowing letters that also draw a conductor, The Partners, early 2000s [SRC-394] |
| **Hidden number or initial** | a numeral or letter tucked into the main shape | a 31 in the coloured parts of an ice-cream chain's BR (2006; designer not verified) [SRC-393]; a 1 in the gap between an F and speed lines, Carter Wong, 1994 [SRC-391] |
| **Hidden animal or emblem** | a figure inside a landscape or object | a standing bear in a chocolate bar's mountain, kept through the 2022 and 2023 redesigns [SRC-387] |
| **Gesture that doubles as meaning** | one stroke carries a second idea | a smile that is also an arrow from a to z, Turner Duckworth, 2000 [SRC-388] |
| **Ambigram / rotation** | the mark reads the same turned round | a computer company's name four times round a square, Vaughan Pratt, 1982 [SRC-390] |
| **Rebus / substitution** | an object replaces a word or letter | a heart for "love", Milton Glaser, 1976 [SRC-403] |

## How the good ones were made

- **Found in the material, not bolted on.** The whale mark began as "a way of thinking": the owner liked a harpoon sketch because it held an H; the designer refused harpoons ("killing your own mascot"), asked for more days, and saw that the whale tail, the W and the H were all symmetrical and could share one shape. He cut and sanded the tail template from acetate [SRC-382]. The arrow came from letterforms blended from two typefaces until it sat naturally [SRC-355]. The nib-spoon replaced an earlier fork idea because the spoon was simpler [SRC-386].
- **Start from the name's parts.** List the nouns in the name and brief (swan, mallard, ampersand; tour, rider, wheel), then hunt for a contour, letter or gap that two of them already share. The duck mark's designer describes uniting the three parts of a restaurant's name and aiming for "a smile in the mind" [SRC-384].
- **Draw both readings at full strength.** Each figure must be a good drawing alone. Then fuse, and fix the joins until neither looks like a compromise.
- **Test the order.** The main reading must land in under two seconds; the second should arrive on a second look or when pointed out (LOGO-8).
- **Keep the hidden part big enough.** Negative-space figures shrink fastest. A sports body retired its hidden-1 mark partly because it did not hold up on screens [SRC-391].

## Failure modes

- **Forced:** one figure is a weak drawing propping up the other. Redditors noted the Spartan's plume and the golf swing don't quite match [SRC-383]; trend reports flag plugged counters as "occasionally forced" [SRC-358].
- **Invisible:** many fans did not see the whale mark's H for years [SRC-382]; a hidden reading nobody finds is decoration, not meaning.
- **Unintended third reading:** one designer posting a negative-space mark found viewers saw a second elephant in the figure's body that was never meant [SRC-411]. Show it to strangers (LOGO-8, LOGO-13).
- **Dies small:** fine gaps close up (LOGO-3).
- **Cleverness instead of fit:** a trick unrelated to the brand reads as a puzzle, not a promise.
- **Hard to redraw:** later teams may simplify the mark and lose the trick; the rider mark was redrawn to read more easily [SRC-385].

## Why people love them

| Claim | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Discovering a hidden figure is pleasurable | the "aha" is a sudden jump in processing ease, felt as pleasure and as a sense of truth [SRC-380]; applying it to logos is an inference | theory with support; inference for logos |
| Figure-ground logos raise brand attitude | a 2026 set of studies (bidding, willingness to pay, eye-tracking) found reversal logos raised attitude through engagement and aesthetic appeal, **only when** the hidden object could be identified and given meaning [SRC-378] | partly supported (one paper, several studies) |
| The hidden part works unconsciously | no: in cueing experiments a famous hidden arrow did not guide attention unless made explicit, and most viewers did not report it [SRC-379] | not supported; the bonus needs noticing |
| Active space improves ratings | adding space between parts of pictorial logos improved evaluations [SRC-381] | partly supported |
| Simpler is always better | moderately elaborate marks were remembered better than very simple ones [SRC-359] | partly supported |

**Bottom line:** the second reading is a reward for attention, and a story people retell. It does not work on people who never see it, so the mark must work without it (LOGO-8), and the second figure must be clear and relevant once seen (LOGO-16).

## Stories to check before retelling

- **The Spartan golf mark** appears to be a self-initiated concept rather than a real club's identity: it sits in the designer's portfolio and a logo gallery, and a commenter on the viral thread says the club is not real [SRC-383]. Present it as concept work.
- **The swan and mallard** is a London designer's work, though social posts often misplace it [SRC-384].
- **Ice-cream 31:** the hidden number is real; the 2006 designer is not reliably credited [SRC-393].
- **Zoo tree:** the designer is uncredited and the mark is retired [SRC-392].
