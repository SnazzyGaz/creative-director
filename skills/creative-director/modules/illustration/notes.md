# Illustration notes (optional depth)

Background for the rules in `README.md`. Read when a critique needs more vocabulary. Nothing here is a separate rule.

## Itten's seven colour contrasts [S48]
A checklist of ways colours can differ. Use it to name *which* contrast is doing the work at the focal point (CORE-3):
1. Hue: pure, distinct hues.
2. Light-dark: value. This is usually the strongest (CORE-2).
3. Cold-warm: temperature.
4. Complementary: opposites, which intensify each other side by side and grey each other when mixed.
5. Simultaneous: a colour pushes its neighbour toward its own complement (Albers' "one colour looks like two" [S47]).
6. Saturation: pure against dulled.
7. Extent: area. A small intense patch balances a large muted one.

## Describing colour precisely
- Munsell splits colour into hue, value (0 black to 10 white) and chroma (distance from grey), in roughly equal visual steps [S49]. Good vocabulary for critiques: "raise value one step, lower chroma".
- On screens, OKLCH does the same with perceptual lightness. Use it for "same lightness, different hue" checks. HSL's lightness is not perceptual [S50].

## Simplification
- McCloud: cartooning is "amplification through simplification". Removing detail makes an image more universal and readable, and the viewer fills the gaps (closure) [S81]. This supports CORE-8 and CORE-10 for stylised work.
- In sequences, the gap between panels is where the reader does the work. Bigger jumps (scene to scene) ask more of them [S81].

## Loomis's tonal plans [S76]
Simplify the picture to four values (white, light grey, dark grey, black), pick which one is the ground, and set the subject against it. Thumbnail the plan before any detail.

## Light and shadow vocabulary
Robertson & Bertling's *How to Render* builds form from light, shadow and reflectivity: cast-shadow construction in perspective, and how matte and glossy surfaces differ [S72]. Gurney's *Color and Light* covers the colour side [S51].
